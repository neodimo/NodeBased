# Volumes and liquids at interactive speed

**Lane 3 R5 · research date 2026-10-20.** Documentation/source inspection only. No weights downloaded, application changes, renderer execution or graphics-device work. Living sources below were accessed on this date; publication dates are stated when established. This is a scoped survey, not a latency leaderboard.

## Evidence and the question being answered

The R5 assignment (2026-10-20) supplies **26.69 s** to a **480 × 270, 1-sample** Hot pour image in a **1920 × 1080** viewport, and **112–756 s** for **960 × 540, 8–128 sample** frames on the **12 GB RTX 3080 Ti**, using a **64-cell** liquid surface and steam. These are **assignment-reported measurements, not independently verified here**. The inspected main revision, `15e28165`, still says the corrected sample matrix and viewport timings are outstanding in [FLUIDS_SPIKE, S2](FLUIDS_SPIKE.md#s2-of-2-the-hot-pour-reads-as-a-pour-in-progress-2026-10-15). Its corrected stills took **200.04–201.88 s at 32 samples**, and the older two-cup sweep describes a different composition. Recover the newer raw timing record before comparative acceptance; do not silently replace either run.

**Targets from the R5 assignment, 2026-10-20:** a fresh, useful warm first look **under 1 s**, then a settled image **under 60 s** on that card. Relative to the supplied times, the required improvements exceed **26.69×** for first look and **1.87–12.6×** for the frame range (derived by dividing by the target seconds; these are required reductions, not forecasts). First displayed approximation, first unbiased sample, final quality and cold preparation are different clocks. A stale image or an invisible completed array cannot pass the first-look gate.

## Part 1 — What production renderers actually do

No inspected source establishes a universal millisecond first image for arbitrary refractive liquids plus participating media on our card. The useful pattern is persistent preparation, an explicitly cheaper image during interaction, then progressively more faithful sampling. Even production products have cold stalls.

### Preparation and simulation-frame reuse

**Engineering synthesis from the sources above:** separate scene identity from camera identity. Keep mesh bottom-level acceleration, sparse density/temperature topology, conservative extinction bounds and device allocations resident across camera moves. Update instance transforms/top-level acceleration when needed. Liquid topology may change every simulated frame: refitting yesterday's triangle hierarchy is only valid while connectivity is unchanged and quality remains adequate. Changed topology requires rebuilding the affected hierarchy. Materials affecting extinction invalidate the relevant majorants; lights invalidate radiance, not necessarily geometry.

Persist independently addressable render artifacts with each simulation frame: surface topology/vertices, sparse fields, bounds, and proxy levels. A bounded adjacent-frame cache can hide decoding; it cannot reuse last frame's radiance as a converged result after density, geometry or lighting changes. Account for decode, upload and duplicate host/device copies. Offline conversion to NanoVDB may help repeat access but does not make conversion free or give WGSL a native NanoVDB reader.

### Coarse passes, skipping and tracking

**Density mips** lower sampling bandwidth for a deliberately approximate preview. Preserve thin steam and integrated extinction; averaged density is not a safe upper bound for rejection sampling. **Majorant hierarchies** instead store conservative extinction maxima including interpolation neighborhoods; traverse empty cells directly and sample tighter nonempty regions. These are different hierarchies and must not be substituted for one another. **Delta tracking** proposes extinction events and rejects null collisions; **ratio tracking** estimates transmittance with weighted null events. Tighter bounds reduce wasted density queries but traversal has a cost, and a high-density outlier can spoil a coarse cell. Deterministic shadow marching trades bias for bounded cost. Count work and image error before replacing it.

The production docs [N1] and papers [P1, P2] support these distinctions. Locally they suggest profiling shadow transmittance and existing majorant quality before implementing another sparse format. Bounds must cover temperature-driven emission even where density is low; skipping solely by density can erase fire. A coarse preview must retain the liquid silhouette, refraction/absorption cue and visible steam, while explicitly postponing expensive multiple scattering or caustics.

### Progressive samples, convergence and denoising

Accumulate independent samples into persistent sums/moments for an unchanged view; distribute work to uncertain pixels. A variance-derived standard error is an estimator, not proof that rare refractive paths or caustics have been observed. Require minimum samples, coverage checks and a high-quality reference; distinguish stopped-by-budget from converged. All image-space history must reset or be validity-tested after edits. Denoised output must never feed back into the unbiased accumulator.

**OIDN and OptiX at 1 spp:** [D1, D2] support fast denoising, but do not certify faithful single-sample smoke, glass or caustics for this shot. At one sample a path may miss a thin wisp entirely; filtering cannot recover reliable evidence. Surface guides can describe the glass boundary while the visible signal lies behind it. Compare beauty-only and appropriate albedo/normal guides, with explicit background/alpha handling. Keep raw output available and test temporal stability, steam retention and refracted detail. OIDN exposes CPU and multiple vendor device backends, persistent filters, quality choices and capability-dependent external-buffer sharing [D1]. OptiX is NVIDIA-specific [D2]. Their initialization, guide generation, transfers and inference belong in the measured budget; no matched 12 GB Ampere latency or memory peak was found in these sources. Neither is a drop-in WGSL operation.

### Recent papers and what to defer

| Paper, publication date | Transferable idea | Why this is not an immediate speed promise |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Progressive null-tracking**, SIGGRAPH **2023** [P1] | Consistent progressive treatment of initially unknown extinction bounds, plus improved transmittance importance sampling. | Useful for procedural media; our voxel grids can compute bounds directly. Replacing a proven bounded-grid estimator is a correctness-heavy task. No matched target-card cost. |
| **Beyond ExaBricks**, CGF **2024** [P2] | Compare spatial accelerators and majorants for volume path tracing; acceleration suited to marching need not suit sparse stochastic events. | AMR scientific volumes differ from the app's tiled fluids. Profile traversal versus density fetches first. No matched Hot pour/12 GB cost. |
| **RenderMan XPU**, HPG **2025** [P3] | Progressive CPU/GPU renderer architecture and interactive work scheduling. | Search-index abstract verified; the old full-paper URL redirected to Pixar's library landing page. No paper benchmark claimed from that redirect; product mechanisms above use accessible R1/R2 instead. |
| **MRBNN**, SIGGRAPH **2026** [P4] | Learned radiance baking for real-time volume appearance. | Author project and code discovered in this sweep; changing simulation fields, preparation/training costs, arbitrary refraction and target-card fit remain unverified. Defer; no weights downloaded. |
| **Fast Volume Rendering with Spatiotemporal Reservoir Resampling**, **2021-11** [P5], foundational comparator | Reuse useful lighting samples across space/time. | Dynamic disocclusion and changing density demand validity/reweighting. Adds reservoirs and complex estimator tests; postpone until basic preparation and sample reuse are measured. No local speed factor inferred. |
